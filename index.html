Overview

In this homework I built a path tracer that renders realistic images by simulating how light bounces around a scene. It shoots rays from the camera, finds what they hit, and uses a BVH to make that fast enough for meshes with over 100,000 triangles. On top of that I added direct lighting, global illumination with Russian roulette, and adaptive sampling. The most interesting thing I learned is that most of the work in rendering is fighting noise and wasted effort: the biggest improvements came from skipping work that doesn't matter and aiming samples where they actually help.

Part 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection

Generating rays

For each pixel, I send ns_aa rays through random spots in the pixel and average the colors they bring back. Using several random rays instead of one helps smooth out jagged edges.

To make a ray, I imagine a screen one unit in front of the camera, sized by the field of view. The pixel position (scaled from 0 to 1) tells me where on the screen to aim, so the ray goes from the camera through that point. I rotate that direction from camera space into world space with c2w, normalize it, and only count hits between the near and far clipping planes.

Finding what a ray hits

Each ray remembers the farthest distance it still cares about (max_t). Whenever it hits something, max_t shrinks to that distance, so anything farther away gets ignored. After checking everything, the ray is left with the closest hit. I save the distance, the normal, the object, and its material.

Triangles

I used the Möller–Trumbore algorithm. Any point on a triangle is a mix of its three corners, using two weights \(b_1\) and \(b_2\). Setting that equal to the ray \(\mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d}\) gives three equations for \(t\), \(b_1\), and \(b_2\), which Möller–Trumbore solves all at once:

\[ \begin{bmatrix} t \\ b_1 \\ b_2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{\mathbf{s}_1 \cdot \mathbf{e}_1} \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{s}_2 \cdot \mathbf{e}_2 \\ \mathbf{s}_1 \cdot \mathbf{s} \\ \mathbf{s}_2 \cdot \mathbf{d} \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{e}_1 = \mathbf{p}_2 - \mathbf{p}_1,\ \mathbf{e}_2 = \mathbf{p}_3 - \mathbf{p}_1,\ \mathbf{s} = \mathbf{o} - \mathbf{p}_1,\ \mathbf{s}_1 = \mathbf{d} \times \mathbf{e}_2,\ \mathbf{s}_2 = \mathbf{s} \times \mathbf{e}_1 \]

It's a hit if both weights are at least 0, they add up to at most 1, and \(t\) is in range. The normal is a blend of the three corner normals using the same weights, which makes curved meshes look smooth.

Spheres

Plugging the ray into the sphere equation gives a quadratic in \(t\). No real solution means a miss. Otherwise I take the closer solution that's in range. The normal points from the center to the hit point.

Part 2: Bounding Volume Hierarchy

Building the BVH

A BVH is a tree of boxes. Each node has a box around some triangles, and its two children split those triangles into two groups. I build it recursively. I put a box around the current triangles, and if there are only a few (max_leaf_size or fewer), I make a leaf and stop. Otherwise I split them in two and repeat on each half.

To split, I look at the center of each triangle's box, find the axis where the centers are most spread out, and cut at their average. Triangles below the cut go left and the rest go right. Cutting along the longest direction keeps the two boxes from overlapping much. If everything ends up on one side, I just split the list in half so the recursion can't go on forever.

Intersecting the BVH

For a ray and a box, I find when the ray enters and leaves the box along each axis. The ray is inside the box only when all three of those time ranges overlap.

To trace a ray through the tree, I start at the top. If the ray misses a box, I skip everything inside it. At a leaf, I test its triangles. Otherwise I check both children. If I just need to know whether anything is hit, like for shadow rays, I stop at the first hit. If I need the closest hit, I check both sides.

Part 3: Direct Illumination

First I wrote the diffuse BSDF. A diffuse surface spreads light evenly in all directions, so its BSDF is just its color divided by \(\pi\). I also added zero-bounce lighting, which is light given off directly by light sources. That's what makes the ceiling light show up.

Hemisphere sampling

At each hit point, I shoot rays in random directions over the half-sphere above the surface. If a ray hits a light, I add BSDF × light brightness × \(\cos\theta\), divided by the chance of picking that direction (\(1/2\pi\)). Then I average over all the rays. New rays start a tiny bit off the surface so they don't hit the spot they came from.

Light sampling

Most random directions miss the light, so instead I aim straight at the lights. For each light, I pick a point on it, make sure it's in front of the surface, and send a shadow ray to check that nothing is in the way. If it's clear, I add the same BSDF × brightness × \(\cos\theta\) / probability term. Point lights get one sample, since every sample would be the same. Area lights get several, which I average.

Number of light samples

1 sample per pixel, light sampling:

With 1 light sample, the bunny's shadow is very speckled, because each pixel either sees the light or it doesn't. More light samples let each pixel average over more of the light, so the shadow gets smoother. At 64 samples, it's a clean, soft shadow that fades gradually at the edges.

Comparison

Light sampling looks much cleaner for the same number of samples. With hemisphere sampling, most rays never reach the light, so they're wasted and the image stays grainy. With light sampling, almost every ray is aimed at a light, so the noise drops a lot and the shadows look smoother. It also works with point lights, which hemisphere sampling can't handle.

Part 4: Global Illumination

How it works

In Part 3, light could only go from a light to a surface to the camera. Now it can bounce around the room first. At each hit point, I still add the direct light from Part 3. Then I pick a new direction from the BSDF, shoot a ray that way, and if it hits something, I do the same thing again there. The light that comes back gets the usual BSDF × \(\cos\theta\) / probability factor and is added in.

Each ray has a depth that starts at the max depth (-m) and drops by one with every bounce. When it reaches 1, I stop. With -o 0, I only keep the light from the last bounce instead of adding them all up, so each bounce can be viewed on its own.

To avoid tracing every path all the way to the max depth, I used Russian roulette. After the first bounce, each path keeps going with probability 0.65. When it does, I divide its light by 0.65 to make up for the paths that stopped, so the image stays equally bright on average. I always do at least one indirect bounce, and a path also ends if its ray leaves the scene.

Everything below uses 1024 samples per pixel and 4 light samples unless noted otherwise.

Global illumination

Direct vs. indirect lighting

Direct lighting alone looks harsh: the ceiling is black and the shadows are completely dark, since only spots that can see the light get lit. Indirect lighting is light that bounced off something else first. It's softer, it fills in the ceiling and the shadows, and it carries red and blue from the walls onto the spheres and floor. Together they make the full image.

One bounce at a time (-o 0)

Bounce 0 is just the light, and bounce 1 is direct lighting. The 2nd bounce is the first that shows indirect light. The ceiling, the bunny's underside, and the shadows light up from light reflecting off the floor and walls, and the wall colors tint nearby surfaces. The 3rd bounce is dimmer and even softer, since the light has been spread around twice. Each bounce after that gets darker, because surfaces absorb some light every time. These extra bounces are what make the image look real. Rasterization usually only does direct lighting plus a flat "ambient" term, so it can't show soft light filling in shadows or colors bleeding between surfaces.

Adding up bounces (-o 1)

Each image adds one more bounce. The biggest change is from 1 to 2 bounces, when the ceiling and shadows go from black to lit. After that, each bounce only brightens things a little, and by 4 or 5 bounces the image barely changes.

Russian roulette

With Russian roulette, a max depth of 100 looks almost the same as a max depth of 4 (just a tiny bit brighter), and it rendered about as fast as a max depth of 5: 183 s either way. Most paths get stopped long before 100 bounces, so a high max depth stays cheap.

Samples per pixel

At 1 sample per pixel, the image is very noisy, because each pixel comes from just one random path. More samples means less noise. At 64 samples, it's clear but still a bit grainy in the shadows and on the ceiling, which only get indirect light. At 1024 samples, it's smooth.

Part 5: Adaptive Sampling

How it works

Some pixels clean up after just a few samples, like the light or a brightly lit wall, while others, like shadows and the ceiling, stay noisy for a long time. Adaptive sampling stops taking samples for a pixel once it looks good enough, so more of the time goes to the pixels that actually need it.

For each pixel, I keep two running totals as samples come in: the sum of each sample's brightness, \(s_1\), and the sum of each brightness squared, \(s_2\). After every samplesPerBatch samples, I use them to get the mean \(\mu\) and variance \(\sigma^2\):

\[ \mu = \frac{s_1}{n}, \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\left(s_2 - \frac{s_1^2}{n}\right), \qquad I = 1.96 \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \]

\(I\) tells me how far off the pixel's average could still be (with 95% confidence). If \(I \le\) maxTolerance \(\cdot\ \mu\), the pixel has settled, so I stop and move on. Otherwise I keep going, up to the maximum (-s). I save how many samples each pixel took, which is what the sample rate images show.

Results

Max 2048 samples per pixel, batches of 64, tolerance 0.05, 1 light sample, max depth 5. In the rate images, red means a pixel used a lot of samples and blue means it stopped early.

The ceiling, the shadows under the objects, and the bottom of the bunny are red. Those areas only get indirect light, which is noisy, so they needed close to the full 2048 samples. The light itself and the walls facing it are blue, because they're lit directly and settle quickly. Each scene took about 65 seconds, and the final images still come out clean, since the samples went where the noise was.
